# The Neo-Riemannian Theory of Music

The Neo-Riemannian Theory of Music (NRT) is a loose collections of research and ideas concering the triad, its transformations, and a lattice-based representation of all possible variants called the tonnetz. The triad itself is a particular chord consisting of three elements that satisfy certain intervallic constraints. More specifically, the _root_ of the triad is a pitch class that is the basis for the chord while the _third_ defines the chord's quality (major or minor) and the _fifth_ provides stability to the chord. The triad can be represented as a set of pitch classes, for example, the C major triad can be represented as {C, E, G} or in integer notation as {0, 4, 7}. When considering the triad as a set of three notes it becomes apparent that the distace between the first and second note as well as between the second and third note is either three or four semitones. To create a _perfect_ fifth one must alternate between the two variants of a third (3 or 4 semitones), however, if the two intervals are the same (3 and 3 or 4 and 4 semitones) the result is a diminished or augmented triad, respectively. 

## Transformations

Triads can be transformed using any one of three transformations, namely: leading (L), parallel (P), and relative (R). Each transformation alters one note of the triad while keeping the other two notes constant. The leading transformation (L) moves the root of the triad up or down by a semitone, the parallel transformation (P) changes the quality of the triad from major to minor or vice versa by moving the third up or down by a semitone, and the relative transformation (R) moves the fifth of the triad up or down by two semitones to create a new triad that shares two notes with the original triad.

## Tonnetz

The tonnetz is a lattice-based representation of tonal space initially discussed by Leonhard Euler in 1739 and successfully generalized by Dmitri Tymoczko in 2012. The tonnetz is constructed by arranging pitch classes in a two-dimensional grid where each pitch class is connected to its neighbors by intervals of a perfect fifth, major third, and minor third. This arrangement allows for the visualization of triads and their transformations as movements within the lattice. For example, moving horizontally in the tonnetz corresponds to a perfect fifth transformation, while moving diagonally corresponds to major or minor third transformations.
